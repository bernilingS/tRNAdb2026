\PFS{There has been increasing interest in tRNAs promoted in particular by
  an increasingly broad portfolio of roles beyond their primary function of
  mediating the genetic code. Some 10,000 publications now link tRNAs with
  various aspects of cancer, and similar number is concerned with the
  evolution of tRNAs and their processing.  A solid, well-curated body of data
  is therefore is a key resource for an increasing research community.}
Since the publication of the first ``Compilation of tRNA sequences and
sequences of tRNA genes'' in 2005 \cite{sprinzl:2005} and its extension as
the \texttt{tRNAdb} 2009\cite{juehling:2009}, a number of databases either
dedicated to or containing tRNA sequences and genomic locations have been
published (\cite{gtrnadb, trnadb-ce, modomics}. \PFS{When access to the
  \texttt{tRNAdb} was interrupted due to technical issues in 2024/2025, the
  data were still widely used and we received a large number of requests
  for its continued existence. We not only re-implemented the
  \texttt{tRNAdb} on a solid technical foundation, we also included an API for automatic data retrievance. Furthermore, we extended its content by leveraging existing nuclear tRNAs from the gtRNA database as well as mitochondrial, archaeal and phage derived tRNAs we identified using \texttt{mitos2}\cite{mitos2}, \texttt{tRNAscan-SE 2.0}\cite{tRNAscan} and additional specialized approaches.}

\section{Inclusion of new data}\label{sec3}

\subsection{Mitochondrial tRNAs}\label{subsecmitotRNA}

To expanded the mitochondrial tRNAs included into tRNAdb, we downloaded a
total of 16,630 mitochondrial genome sequences as well as their annotations
from NCBI Nucleotide \url{https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/nucleotide/} (downloaded October 2024). We used \texttt{Mitos2}\cite{mitos2} without detection of proteins and rRNA and the refseq89m models on 
each mitochondrion to detect the tRNAs. We then compared the predicted
sequences with the GenBank annotation. All tRNAs that matched exactly were
added to the \texttt{trnaDB}. In case of small discrepancies of location,
we checked whether the structure of the acceptor stem predicted by
\texttt{infernal} passed human curation. If so, the sequence predicted by
\cite{mitos2} was deemed correct and included into the database. In case of discrepancies in the assignment of the strand (reading direction), we retained the
\cite{mitos2} prediction if no plausible anticodon could be identified
in the anticodon stem and loop of the annotated Refseq entry.
In total, this resulted in  \todo{a number} of novel predicted mtRNAs that were
added to the \texttt{tRNAdb}. 

\subsection{Nuclear tRNAs}\label{subsecgenomictRNA}
For genomic tRNAs, we downloaded sequences, locations and genome ids from
the gtRNA database\cite{gtrnadb}. 


\subsection{Archaeal tRNAs}

Archaeal genomes were sourced from the  BV-BRC database \url{https://www.bv-brc.org}. Predictions were ran on good quality genomes, as assessed by CheckM2 ($\geq$85\% complete, $<$5\% contaminated)\cite{checkm2}. These archaeal genomes cover a broad taxonomic range and include all major archaeal superphyla (Asgard, DPANN, TACK, and Euryarchaeota).
We did not rely on the BV-BRC annotation of tRNA genes, as we found it inconsistent with predictions using the most up-to-date version of \texttt{tRNAscan-SE 2.0.12}\cite{tRNAscan}. Notably, tRNA-Ile2 is often mislabelled as tRNA-Met in BV-BRC. This is the most frequent mismatch we observed (34\%). This likely reflects the agmatidine wobble-base modification unique to archaea \cite{tRNAscan}, highlighting the necessity to use domain specific models.
Therefore all genomes were annotated using {\texttt{tRNAscan-SE 2.0.12}} in archaeal mode. Selenocysteine tRNAs were identified using the SeC-specific covariance model, since it has an atypical secondary structure poorly captured by general domain specific models \cite{tRNAscan}. As independent validation, all tRNAscan predictions were re-scored with cmsearch 1.1.5 \cite{infernal}, against tRNAinf-arch and tRNAinf-arch-SeC models. 
Overall, tRNA genes from 228 unique archaeal genomes were added, of which 214 were not represented in the 2009 tRNAdb, substantially expanding archaeal coverage relative to the legacy dataset.


\subsection{Phage tRNAs} 
Complete genome sequences of all viruses annotated with the host term ``Bacteria'' were downloaded from the NCBI Virus \url{https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/labs/virus/vssi/#/}; accessed 17 August 2026). Putative tRNA genes
were identified using tRNAscan-SE v2.0.12 in bacterial mode. Predicted tRNAs were retained only if they were 70–90 nt in length, had an overall tRNAscan-SE score >60, and had both HMM and secondary-structure scores >10. Predictions with undefined isotypes or classified as pseudogenes were excluded.


\subsection{modified tRNAs}\label{subsecmodifiedtRNA}
Modified tRNA sequences as well as their genomic variants were downloaded
from the MODOMIC\cite{modomics} database and directly from the pdb. We
removed sequences from the pdb that \PFS{had only a single
  modification} located at the first or last base of the
retrieved sequence. \PFS{In order display alignments properly} we
decided to keep legacy one letter codes for modifications.  
We \PFS{extended the dictionary of one letter codes} for modifications not
already present in tRNAdb 2009. For these modifications, we also identified the name of the compound using the  pdb entry. We then extracted InChikeys and InChis from PubChem \cite{pubchem2025} to  check whether the compound was already present in the MODOMIC database. A list of all modifications with the one letter codes used in tRNAdb and MODOMIC \TODO{can be found in the supplement and on the database page}.
Modified mitochondrial tRNA sequences from \emph{h.sapiens}\cite{humanmtRNA} and \emph{b.taurus}\cite{modtRNAbtaurus} where added from the supplement of the respective publications. The included canonical base numberings were directly used for alignment and structure annotation. 




\subsection{Alignemnt and quality control}
For all novel sequences, we used \texttt{infernal} \TODO{version, ref} to align sequences to
the respective tRNA covariance models (eukaryotic, bacterial or archaeal
models from tRNAscan for nuclear tRNAs, the respective Mitos2 models for
mitochondrial tRNAs). When an anticodon was annotated, we compared the
annotated one with the one implicitly found by infernal (as center of the
anticodon loop). To filter tRNAs with suspicious structural features, we also checked the length of the D-loops, AC-loops, T-loops, as well as the number of unpaired bases between D-stem and AC-stem. For the AC-loop, we used a strict length of 7 nt. For the other structural features, we took the maximum length of the respective structural feature in tRNAdb 2009, added 1 (resulting in 18 and 28 nt for D and T loops respectively and 5 nt between D and AC stem) and discarded a total of \todo{404} tRNAs that did not make one of the four cut-offs.

\section{Implementation}

\subsection{Infrastructure and Implementation}

tRNAdb 2.0 was completely reimplemented as a three tier application consisting of a \texttt{MariaDB} relational database, a \texttt{flask} API backend, and a \texttt{CSS},\texttt{HTML}, and \texttt{javascript} based web frontend. This architecture enables independent change of individual components in order to ensure simple maintenance and future extensions. In contranst to the 2009 implementation of tRNA exclusively in Java and HTML/CSS, this update places tRNAdb up to date with modern software design philosophies. Notably, the tRNAdb website utilizes the public API endpoint exclusively for searching and retrieving data. To provide reliable download of large datasets, tRNAdb supports asynchronous data compilation and transfer via dedicated export threads as well as progress reporting of ongoing export jobs via polling.

\subsection{Data model and provenance}

For easy retrieval and global searches, sequences and their most relevant data are stored within a single, fully indexed relation enabling efficient filtering across all sequence categories and attributes. This includes nucleotide sequence, secondary structure, anticodon, amino acid and origin (i.e., nuclear DNA, nuclear RNA, mitochondrial DNA). Every sequence has a taxonomic identifier and a separate table holds relevant taxonomic information which is obtained from the NCBI taxonomy database. To facilitate legacy compatibility, the original tRNA dataset \cite{juehling:2009} remains unaltered regarding sequence, structure, anticodon, amino acid, references, and tRNAdb identifier. We solely updated the taxonomic assignment, i.e. species names and NCBI taxonomy identifiers, to be consistent with the current NCBI taxonomy database. We used the NCBI taxonomy data dump \TODO{VERSION,DATE} to create an updated local taxonomic table and reconciled legacy taxonomic assignments using \texttt{Bio.entrez} to resolve their current taxonomic information.

For every release, data sources (i.e., origin database and version) and software (with corresponding version) used are documented. Additionally, every tRNAdb release is described with a public version string that is exposed on the ``Details'' page of individual entries, and is also available as an export field. The public version string contains a short description how the sequences published in this particular release were obtained. \TODO{A more detailed description, e.g., software and datasources is available on the ``Documentation and Help'' page of tRNAdb. To enable a sustainable update procedure, we implemented a graphical user interface supported pipeline for import, normalization, and duplicate detection for subsequent tRNAdb versions.

\subsection{API and programmatic access}

With this update we provide a new public HTTP interface, accessible by POST JSON requests at \texttt{https://tdb.bioinf.uni-leipzig.de/api}. The API implements \TODO{OpenAPI} specifications and a thourough documentation for users is available on the ``Documentation'' page of the tRNAdb website. Fundamentally, our API supports nearly identical search parameters as the tRNAdb website and allows filtering by any combination of origin, species, clade, NCBI taxonomy ID, amino acid, anticodon, sequence and structural motifs, reference (genome accession, external source, publication), and \TODO{version}. For many fields, multiple values can be supplied and entries matching at least one are returned. Individual records can be precisely retrieved by using the stable identifier \texttt{trna\_id} which also remained unaltered for the 2009 legacy dataset.

No prior registration, and no personal information (e.g., email address, API key) has to be provided to access the tRNAdb API but user requests are limited to one request per second and at most 1000 entries are returned per request to ensure fair use. Every API response, given the request was valid, contains a \texttt{total\_results} field that states the total number of sequences matching a request and all relevant results can be retrieved paginated via an offset parameter. For requests that match a large number of records, hence bulk retrieval of tRNA sequences, a second API endpoint provides export to tsv, csv, json, or fasta which is available at \texttt{https://tdb.bioinf.uni-leipzig.de/export}. \TODO{For usage details see the documentation page of the tRNAdb website.} \TODO{OpenAPI, /api/v1, stable-ID retrieval, canonical URLs, documentation}


\subsection{Website}

The tRNAdb website (see Figure \ref{Fig:Website}) can be found at \url{https://tdb.bioinf.uni-leipzig.de/}. It provides user-friendly access to the tRNA data stored in our database and can be functionally separated into three subpages: ``Search'', ``Details'', and ``Taxonomy Tree''.
\begin{figure}
  \includegraphics[width=0.45\textwidth]{Webserver_homo_ala.png}
  \caption{Example for the tRNAdb website, showing the first page of the results for a search for \emph{homo sapiens} Alanine tRNAs\label{Fig:Website}}
\end{figure}
The main functionality of the website is the ``search´´ page which allows users to filter tRNA by organism, origin (e.g., nuclear DNA, nuclear RNA, mitochondrial DNA) amino acid, anticodon, membership of taxonomic clades, external references (publications, genome IDs, external datasources) as well as sequence motifs and structural constraints. Species can be filtered precisely via NCBI taxonomy ID as well as their scientific species name and species synonyms as annotated in the NCBI taxonomy database. For every species with tRNA sequences present in tRNAdb, their taxonomic lineage, as annotated in the NCBI taxonomy database, is stored and tRNA belonging to a specific clade can be obtained by using the ``Clade´´ field of the search page. 

Upon a successful search, the user is presented with a paginated list of fitting tRNA sequences with a snapshot of data available for every sequence: origin, organism, amino acid, anticodon and its structurally annotated nucleotide sequence (separated into acceptor stem, D-loop, D-stem, etc.). Every result entry, or every entry matching the current search request, can be stored in a temporary selection which can be committed to persistent storage as a named collection for later access or download. 

A download manager allows to export individual collections, or every saved collection, and offers customizable export options. Export of data in \texttt{.tsv}, \texttt{.csv}, \texttt{JSON}, and multi line fasta format is supported and every export can be further customized by selecting various fields that are relevant to the user (e.g. sequence, structure, annotated sequence, species name, taxonomic lineage, reference, and more). Fasta export supports a customizable sequence identifier, for which a preview is generated.

Furthermore, a standardized visualization of the secondary structure with a highlighted anticodon is displayed for every entry.  %The visualizations were generated using a custom made script employing R2R\cite{R2R}.

We implemented a searchable taxonomy browser which displays an expandable tree view of the NCBI taxonomic database. Every node in the taxonomy tree is annotated with its scientific name according to NCBI taxonomy as well as its unique NCBI taxonomy ID. The taxonomy browser only displays clades and species, for which there are tRNA sequences present in tRNAdb. For every entry in the taxonomy browser, the number of tRNA sequences separated by origin (nuclear DNA, nuclear RNA, mitochondrial DNA) is displayed. Every node in the taxonomy browser contains a link, which allows to search tRNAdb for sequences belonging to this clade/species.

\section{Statistics}

Duplicated sequences are treated on a species level, hence the same tRNA sequence can occur multiple times in tRNAdb for different species. Thus, besides the total number of entries, we also report the number of unique tRNA sequences included in the database. For mitochondrial tRNAs,  330,261 total entries are comprised of 219,388 unique sequences. The vast majority of these originate in metazoa (see Fig \ref{Tax}a), which reflects the distribution of mitochondrial genomes in RefSeq. The genomic part of the database contains a total of 228,955 entries comprising 93,257 unique sequences. The majority, precisely 149,749 non-unique sequences, are bacterial tRNAs. However, the difference of genomic tRNA representation in tRNAdb is much smaller for unique sequences, see Fig~\ref{Tax}b.

  \caption{\label{Tax}Distribution of taxa. a: mitochondrial tRNAs at kingdom level. b: genomic tRNAs at domain level.}
\end{figure}

The most abundant tRNA in the genomic tRNAdb part is Leu with 23,995 entries and 10,233 unique sequences. Besides the non-standard Suppressor and SeC tRNAs, His has the fewest entries with 5,290 (but 2,305 unique sequences), while Phe and Trp have the fewest unique sequences with 2,093 (but 5,855 and 5,579 entries, respectively). Notably, many genomic tRNAs (Asn, Asp, Cys, His, Phe, Trp, Tyr) show a clear preference for one codon (see Fig.~\ref{Aminoacidamounts}a).
Mitochondrial tRNAs show a comparatively even distribution with respect to their abundance, ranging from 14,692 entries for Glu to 16,734 entries for Met, with the exception of Ser1 and Leu1, which are the only amino acids that come in two different forms in many mitochondria. Mitochondrial tRNA for Lys is the only tRNA utilizing a notable amount of a secondary anticodon (Fig~\ref{Aminoacidamounts}b).

%%As a constantly increasing number of complete genomes is published, it becomes necessary to transfer existing sequence compilations to state-of-the-art IT infrastructure to cope with the challenges of the post-genomic era. One of the most abundant groups of nucleic acids are tRNA molecules, being present in all types of cells and organelles. Unique features of these molecules include (i) the high degree of structural conservation, (ii) the plethora of cellular factors tRNAs interact with, and (iii) the largest diversity and highest density of nucleoside modifications found in nature. Furthermore, with respect to phylogeny, tRNAs offer a unique complexity: since each cell and eukaryotic organelle has a cohort of related but distinct tRNA species, phylogenetic analyses permit an integrated view on an entire set of tRNAs that underwent coevolution, rather than being restricted to comparison of a single RNA species. Hence, a tRNA database must fulfil specific criteria in order to address these features. The tRNAdb, with one of the largest numbers of entries among RNA databases, is not only an excellent model system for implementing and validating algorithms and processes of automated nucleic acid data transfer, but also for the development of novel sequence analysis tools (1). In its re-structured version, the tRNAdb meets the demands of present-day web-based interfaces and provides a basis for integration of structure-function relationships and additional information on evolution and phylogeny.
